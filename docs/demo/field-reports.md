## Article 1 — "Three cold mornings on the Deschutes"
*Tags: field-report · Author: [name] · Date: [October 2026]*
*Excerpt: We took the new Storm Wading Jacket through rain, wind, and one
accidental swim. Here is what held up and what we changed before it shipped.*

The Deschutes in late October is not subtle. It rained the first morning, blew the
second, and on the third one of us went in to the waist chasing a hooked fish
downstream. Three days is not a lab, but it is honest.

The shell did its job. Fully-taped seams and waterproof zips kept the rain out
through a six-hour sit, and the pockets cleared a wading belt without riding up.
The one change we made afterward: a longer storm cuff, because water tracked down a
raised arm on the cast. The production jackets have it.

Fishing was a 5-weight game — a Tailout rod, a floating line, and a 4X leader under
an indicator. Nymphs did the work; a Pheasant Tail in #14 outfished everything by
midday. The takeaway was less about flies than about staying warm enough to keep
casting when it would have been easier to quit. A grid-fleece hoodie under the shell
was the difference.

If you fish cold, moving water: dress for the third hour, not the first.

## Article 2 — "Reading a freestone creek in low water"
*Tags: field-report · Author: [name] · Date: [September 2026]*
*Excerpt: When the water drops and clears, the fish do not leave — they get harder.
A short rod, a long leader, and a slow approach.*

Low water is a different sport. The creek we fish most drops through September until
the runs you waded in June are ankle-deep glass. The trout are still there; they are
just spookier, tucked into the last foot of shade and broken surface.

Three things mattered. First, a short rod — a 3- or 4-weight loads at the close range
these fish demand and lands a fly without a splash. Second, a long, fine leader:
9 feet to 5X or 6X, because a #18 dry on 4X in clear water is a warning, not an offer.
Third, patience on the approach — kneel, wait, and cast once well rather than three
times in a hurry.

We fished dries almost entirely: a Parachute Adams and an Elk Hair Caddis, drifted
drag-free into the head of each run. Every fish came in the first good drift or not
at all.

Low water rewards the angler who slows down. There is no rushing a spooky trout in
a foot of clear creek.

## Article 3 (guide) — "Match your leader to the fly, not the rod"
*Tags: guide · Author: [name] · Date: [September 2026]*
*Excerpt: Tippet size is set by the fly, not the rod. The simple rule that keeps
you from spooking fish — or breaking off.*

A common mistake is choosing tippet by rod weight. Tippet is set by the **fly
size**, and the shortcut is simple: divide the hook size by three for a good
starting X. A #12 fly fishes well on 4X (12 ÷ 3 = 4); a #18 wants 6X.

For trout that means: dries and nymphs on **4X–6X**, streamers on **2X–4X**. Go a
size heavier in dirty or windy water and when fish are strong; go finer when the
water is clear and the fish are spooky.

all three and rebuild the tippet end as your fly changes through the day.
