## Article 1 — "Three cold mornings on the Deschutes"
*Tags: field-report · Author: [name] · Date: 2026-05-12*
*Excerpt: We took the Storm Wading Jacket through rain, wind, and one accidental
swim. Here is what held up and what we changed before it shipped.*

The Deschutes in a wet spring is not subtle. It rained the first morning, blew the
second, and on the third one of us went in to the waist chasing a hooked fish
downstream. Three days is not a lab, but it is honest.

The shell did its job. Fully-taped seams and waterproof zips kept the rain out
through a six-hour sit, and the pockets cleared a wading belt without riding up.
The one change we made afterward was a longer storm cuff, because water tracked
down a raised arm on the cast — the production jackets have it.

Fishing was a 5-weight game: a Tailout rod and a 4X leader under an indicator.
Nymphs did the work, and a Pheasant Tail in #14 outfished everything by midday.
The real lesson was warmth — a grid-fleece hoodie under the shell was the
difference between fishing the third hour and quitting after the first.

Dress for the third hour, not the first.

## Article 2 — "Reading a freestone creek in low water"
*Tags: field-report · Author: [name] · Date: 2026-08-03*
*Excerpt: When the water drops and clears, the fish do not leave — they get
harder. A short rod, a long leader, and a slow approach.*

Low water is a different sport. Our home creek drops through summer until the runs
you waded in June are ankle-deep glass. The trout are still there; they are just
spookier, tucked into the last foot of shade and broken surface.

Three things mattered. First, a short, light rod — a 4-weight Riffle loads at the
close range these fish demand and lands a fly without a splash. Second, a long,
fine leader: 9 feet to 5X or 6X, because a #18 dry on heavy tippet in clear water
is a warning, not an offer. Third, patience on the approach — kneel, wait, and
cast once well rather than three times in a hurry.

We fished dries almost entirely, drifting a Parachute Adams into the head of each
run. Every fish came on the first good drift or not at all.

Low water rewards the angler who slows down.

## Article 3 (guide) — "Match your leader to the fly, not the rod"
*Tags: guide · Author: [name] · Date: 2026-03-20*
*Excerpt: Tippet size is set by the fly, not the rod. The simple rule that keeps
you from spooking fish — or breaking off.*

A common mistake is choosing a leader by rod weight. The turnover end is set by the
**fly size**, and the shortcut is simple: divide the hook size by three for a good
starting X. A #12 fly fishes well on 4X (12 ÷ 3 = 4); a #18 wants 6X.

For trout that means dries and nymphs on **4X–6X**, streamers on **2X–4X**. Go a
size heavier in dirty or windy water and when fish are strong; go finer when the
water is clear and the fish are spooky.

all three and switch as your flies change through the day.
